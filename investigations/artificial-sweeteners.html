SWEETENERS

Artificial Sweeteners: Not as Safe as We Thought

Issue #418 min readMay 7, 2026

You are standing in front of the vending machine at work. It is 3 in the afternoon. You want something sweet, but you do not want the sugar. Your hand hovers over the diet soda. Zero calories. Zero guilt. You press the button and drink it, and you feel like you made the smart choice. For years, I did the same thing. The health world told us these drinks were the safe way to cut back. Now the story is changing, and not in a small way.

What the World Health Organization now says

In May 2023, the World Health Organization put out a new guideline. It said, in plain words: do not use non-sugar sweeteners for weight control. That covers aspartame, sucralose, stevia, and others. The WHO team reviewed the evidence. They found no long-term benefit for body fat, not in adults and not in children. They also said long-term use may bring unwanted effects. Read that again. The product sold to help us lose weight does not help us lose weight.

This was a big turn. For decades, diet drinks and sugar-free snacks were sold as the smart swap. Doctors and diet plans told people to reach for them. The WHO looked at all of it and said the swap does not work for weight control. They did not say these sweeteners are poison. They said the promised benefit is not real, and there may be risks we do not fully understand yet.

The aspartame debate

Then in July 2023, the WHO's cancer research agency, called IARC, looked at aspartame. It placed aspartame in Group 2B. That means "possibly carcinogenic to humans." The agency said the evidence was limited. Limited is an important word. It means they saw some signs, but not enough to be sure. You can read their assessment here.

At the same time, another WHO expert group, JECFA, kept the acceptable daily intake right where it was: 40 mg per kg of body weight per day. That is the amount they say is safe to eat each day. And the US Food and Drug Administration looked at the same evidence and publicly disagreed with the cancer label. Three expert groups. Three different positions. This is how real science works. It is messy, and experts disagree. You deserve to see that disagreement, not a tidy answer that hides it.

What the lab tests show

In 2023, a laboratory study by Schiffman and colleagues looked at sucralose-6-acetate. That is a compound related to sucralose. The researchers tested it on human cells and in lab tests. They found that it damaged DNA. That sounds scary, and it should get our attention. You can read the 2023 study if you want to look it up.

But this was lab research, not people. A cell in a dish is not your gut. Lab findings are a warning flag, not proof of harm in real life. Still, when a lab test raises a flag like this, I do not ignore it. It means we need more real research, and it means caution is fair while we wait.

Where they hide

Sweeteners are not only in diet soda. Once you start reading labels, you will see them everywhere:

  • Sugar-free gum and candy
  • Flavored water and drink mixes
  • Protein shakes and bars
  • Diet and light yogurt
  • Some kids' medicines and vitamins
  • Chewing mints and breath strips

What you can do

First, stop thinking of diet soda as a free pass. It is not water. It may not help your weight at all. Second, read the label. Look for aspartame, sucralose, acesulfame-K (sometimes written as Ace-K), and stevia. They are listed in the ingredients, usually near the end.

Third, if you want sweetness, a small amount of real sugar is at least honest. It is not health food. But you know what it is. Fourth, make water and unsweetened drinks your default. Plain water, sparkling water, unsweetened tea. They are boring. They are also the safest choice we have.
