MSG: The Flavor Enhancer Nobody Admits to Using

Issue #405 min readApril 16, 2026

You are reading a soup label. Big letters on the front say "No added MSG." You feel good about it. Then your eyes drift to the ingredients: "yeast extract." You have a feeling the label is telling you less than the full story — and you might be right.

MSG has one of the strangest stories in the food world. Part scare story, part science, part marketing. Here is the plain version.

By the end of this article, you will know where the scare came from, what the best review of the science found, and how to spot glutamate under its other names.

Where the Scare Started

The whole scare started with a letter. In 1968, someone wrote to the New England Journal of Medicine describing strange symptoms after eating at Chinese restaurants. The letter called it "Chinese restaurant syndrome." You can search for it on PubMed.

One letter — not a study — planted the idea that MSG makes people sick. The name stuck for decades. It also stuck to a whole cuisine in a way that was never fair.

What the Science Actually Says

Here is what the regulators say. The FDA classifies MSG as "generally recognized as safe" — GRAS for short. That is the FDA's category for ingredients it considers safe in food.

In 1995, a group of independent scientists — organized by FASEB — reviewed the evidence for the FDA. Their finding: in double-blind studies, where neither the researchers nor the volunteers knew who got MSG, there was no consistent evidence linking MSG at normal food levels to the reported symptoms.

Double-blind matters because expectations shape how we feel. If you expect a food to make you sick, you are more likely to feel sick after eating it. That does not mean anyone is faking it. It means good studies have to rule that effect out — and the FASEB reviewers looked at studies that did.

Say this honestly, both ways. The studies did not find a clear link at normal food levels. But some people still say they feel bad after eating certain foods with MSG. The evidence is mixed, and your own experience counts for something.

Glutamate Is Already in Your Food

Here is the part that surprises people most. Glutamate — the "G" in MSG — occurs naturally in foods you already love. Tomatoes. Cheese. Mushrooms. The FDA's food pages are a good place to read more about how ingredients like this are regulated.

That does not prove added MSG is fine for everyone. But it does mean the scare was never about a strange foreign chemical.

The Other Names

So why do so many labels brag about "no added MSG"? Because shoppers fear those three letters. Food makers know this. So they use the same flavor under different names.

This is legal. The label is not lying when it says no added MSG, because technically the maker added yeast extract, not MSG. But if you were avoiding MSG, you did not get what you thought you were getting. The FDA's own MSG Q&A confirms the rule: ingredients like yeast extract naturally contain glutamate, and the label does not have to say so.

Watch for these on labels: "yeast extract," "hydrolyzed vegetable protein," and "autolyzed yeast." These ingredients give food that savory taste — without the letters M-S-G anywhere on the package.

What You Can Do

You do not have to pick a side in the MSG wars. You need your own notes.

  • Learn the alias names: yeast extract, hydrolyzed vegetable protein, autolyzed yeast. If a label says "no added MSG" but lists one of these, you are still getting glutamate.
  • If certain foods make you feel off — headache, flushing, a strange feeling — write it down: what you ate, how much, and when. Patterns in your own notes beat internet arguments.
  • Test it fairly. Try the food again another day and see if the same thing happens. One bad evening after a big salty meal proves nothing by itself.
  • Do not trust the hype in either direction. "MSG will poison you" and "MSG is 100 percent safe for everyone" are both stronger claims than the evidence supports.
