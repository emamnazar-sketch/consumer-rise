FATS & OILS

Trans Fats Are Still Here (And Hiding)

Issue #397 min readMarch 26, 2026

You pick up a box of crackers. The front shouts "0g trans fat!" in big letters. It feels like a healthy choice. Then you flip the box and read the ingredients: "partially hydrogenated soybean oil." You wonder how both can be true.

They can both be true because of a labeling rule. This article explains the rule — and why "0g" does not always mean zero.

If that feels misleading, you are not wrong to feel that way. The front of the package is advertising. The ingredient list is the fact sheet.

The FDA Drew a Line

First, the good news. For years, artificial trans fat was everywhere: in margarine, frosting, frozen pizza, microwave popcorn. The main source was partially hydrogenated oils, called PHOs. Food makers used them because they were cheap and kept food fresh longer.

In 2015, the FDA decided that partially hydrogenated oils are no longer "generally recognized as safe" — the GRAS status that lets an ingredient stay in food. Makers had to stop adding PHOs in June 2018, and products already made had until January 2021 to clear the shelves.

January 2020 was the deadline. That was years ago. Most big brands cleaned up their recipes. But smaller brands, imported foods, and old-style products may still carry PHOs. The rule changed the market. It did not erase every trace.

Why the FDA Acted

The FDA acted because trans fats raise LDL cholesterol — the "bad" kind that doctors watch. This is not about one bad meal. Cholesterol builds over years. That is why the FDA looked at the whole food supply instead of single products.

The 0g Loophole

Now the catch. Under US labeling rules, a product can claim "0g trans fat" if it has less than 0.5 grams per serving. Less than half a gram rounds down to zero on the label.

Half a gram sounds like nothing. But servings are small on purpose sometimes. Eat three servings of a snack with 0.4 grams each, and you just ate over a gram of trans fat from a product that says "0g."

Picture a coffee creamer. The serving size is one teaspoon — an amount almost nobody actually uses. Two or three real pours can triple the serving count on the label, and the trans fat triples with it.

That is the loophole: "0g" on the front does not always mean zero in the food.

Why the Loophole Exists

Labeling rules round small numbers. The idea was to keep labels simple. The side effect is that a product can carry artificial trans fat and still advertise zero.

Why "Almost Zero" Still Matters

One product with a trace amount will not hurt you by itself. The problem is the pattern: a little here, a little there, every day, for years. The FDA did not act over one snack. It acted because trans fat was hiding in many foods at once.

What You Can Do

Ignore the front of the package. The truth is on the back. You do not need to memorize chemistry. You need two habits: read the ingredients, and check the serving size. Those two habits catch most of it.

  • Read the ingredients for the words "partially hydrogenated." If those words are there, the product has artificial trans fat — no matter what the front says.
  • Watch the usual hiding spots: margarine, frosting, microwave popcorn, frozen pizza, coffee creamers, and bakery items like pies and pastries.
  • Check the serving size. If a small bag lists three servings, multiply everything by three. That is what you actually eat.
  • When in doubt, pick the simpler product. Fewer ingredients usually means fewer surprises.

This takes about ten seconds per product once you know what to look for. That is a fair trade for knowing what is really in your food.
