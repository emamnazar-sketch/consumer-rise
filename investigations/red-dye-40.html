FOOD DYES

The Hidden Dangers of Red Dye 40

Issue #375 min readFebruary 12, 2026

You know the moment. Your kid is holding a box of something bright red in the grocery store. Cherry candy, a red drink, cereal that looks like it glows. You flip the box over. The ingredient list is long, and there it is: Red 40.

Most of us buy these foods without thinking twice. But there are real questions about this dye, and you deserve to know what they are.

This article covers what Red 40 is, what Europe does about it, and what one well-known study found. No scare tactics. Just the facts, so you can decide for yourself.

What Red 40 Is

Red 40, also called Allura Red AC or E129, is a widely used color additive in American foods, drinks, and candy. It makes food look bright red. It does not add flavor. It does not add nutrition. It is there to make food look fun.

That is the first thing worth knowing: the color is cosmetic. Your child does not need it. It is there to sell the product.

Why Europe Labels It Differently

Here is something that surprises most parents. In the European Union, foods that contain certain artificial dyes — including Allura Red — must carry a warning label. The label says the color may have an adverse effect on activity and attention in children. You can read how Europe handles this on the EFSA food colours page.

Think about that. The same dye sits in American candy with no warning at all. In Europe, parents get a heads-up on the package.

The Southampton Study

The EU rule did not come from nowhere. In 2007, a team led by McCann published a study in The Lancet — the well-known Southampton study. They gave children a mix of artificial colors plus a preservative called sodium benzoate, and they saw more hyperactivity in the kids who drank the mix. You can read the 2007 Southampton study on PubMed.

Be honest about what this means and what it does not. The study tested a mix of colors, not Red 40 alone. It does not prove that Red 40 by itself causes hyperactivity in any given child. But it is a serious study in a serious journal, and it is part of why Europe added the warning.

Regulators Do Act, When the Evidence Builds

Some people say regulators never change their minds. That is not true. In January 2025, the FDA revoked authorization for a different dye, Red No. 3, in food and ingested drugs. Companies have until January 2027 for food and January 2028 for ingested drugs to comply.

That matters because it shows the pattern: evidence builds over years, and then the rules change. Red 40 has not been revoked. But groups like the Center for Science in the Public Interest have been pressing the FDA for years to take a harder look at food dyes. Petitions and pressure from groups like this are part of what moves agencies to act.

The Real Question to Ask

When you see Red 40 on a label, ask one simple question: is this food worth it without the color? If the answer is yes, you can look for a version without the dye. If the answer is no — if the color is the whole point — that tells you something about the food.

What You Can Do

You do not need to panic. You need a plan for the grocery aisle.

  • Look for "Red 40", "Allura Red", or "E129" on ingredient labels. Those are the same dye.
  • The brightest red, orange, and yellow candies, drinks, and cereals are the usual suspects. If it glows, read the label.
  • If your child seems more wired after certain snacks, try cutting the dyed foods for a couple of weeks and see if anything changes. That is your own experiment, not a diagnosis.
  • There are brands that use fruit and vegetable colors instead. They can cost a little more sometimes, but they exist.
