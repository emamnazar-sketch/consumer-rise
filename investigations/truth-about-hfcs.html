SWEETENERS

The Truth About High Fructose Corn Syrup

Issue #4212 min readMay 15, 2026Updated Oct 10, 2026

You grab a cold soda on a hot day. The first sip is perfect. Cold, sweet, sharp. You flip the bottle around. The label says high fructose corn syrup. Those four words have started more dinner-table arguments than almost any other ingredient. Is it poison? Is it just sugar with a bad name? Here is what we actually know.

How it took over

High fructose corn syrup was developed in 1957, when two chemists found a way to turn corn glucose into fructose. It stayed a lab curiosity for years. In the 1970s and 1980s, it took over the American soda supply. Soda makers switched to it. Candy makers switched to it. Soon it was everywhere.

It was not an accident. It was a business decision — and USDA data shows how completely the soda industry switched. By the 1990s, if you drank a soda in America, you were almost certainly drinking corn syrup.

Why the industry loves it

Three reasons. First, it is cheap — that is the main reason it spread so fast. Second, it is a liquid. That makes it easy to mix into soda and other drinks on a fast production line. Third, it keeps products moist on the shelf, so cookies and cakes stay soft longer. The FDA's HFCS questions and answers cover how ingredients like this are regulated.

Put those together and you get an ingredient that saves money at every step. Cheap to buy, easy to use, and it makes products last. For a food company, that is hard to beat. That is why it spread so fast.

Sugar vs. sugar: the real difference

You are in the drink aisle. One soda says "made with real sugar" like that is a health claim. The bottle next to it lists high-fructose corn syrup. You wonder: is one actually better than the other? Short answer: not really.

High fructose corn syrup sounds like a chemical monster. Table sugar sounds natural and safe. The truth is less dramatic. HFCS-55 — the kind in most soda — is about 55 percent fructose and 45 percent glucose. Table sugar, also called sucrose, is 50 percent fructose and 50 percent glucose, with the two bound together. Your body handles them in very similar ways. Once they are digested, the difference is tiny. And here is the part that matters most: neither one is healthy.

Food makers like HFCS because it is cheap and easy to mix into drinks. That is a business reason, not a health reason. Do not confuse the two.

The same goes for honey, agave, and cane sugar, which get marketed as better choices. Your body treats their sugars much the same way. They count toward the same daily limits. Natural on the label does not mean unlimited in your diet. The FDA's HFCS questions and answers are a good starting point if you want to read more about sweeteners.

The peak and the fall

US consumption of high fructose corn syrup peaked around 1999. Then it started to fall. The reason is simple. Soda sales dropped. People started drinking less soda, and the syrup went down with it. Watchdog groups like the Center for Science in the Public Interest pushed hard on soda during those years.

That tells you something important. The story of this ingredient is really the story of soda. When America cut back on soda, the syrup faded too. The ingredient did not get banned. People just bought less of what carried it.

What it does to your body

Here is where we need to be careful. The strongest evidence is not about corn syrup alone. It is about added sugar in general. High intake of added sugar is linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and heart disease. The American Heart Association sets a daily limit: no more than 36 grams of added sugar for men, and 25 grams for women. For children ages 2 to 18, the American Heart Association advises less than 25 grams a day. That is the ceiling, not the goal.

Most Americans blow past it without knowing. 3 in 5 Americans over age 2 take in more added sugar than recommended, says the CDC — and the sugar hides in bread, sauces, yogurt, and drinks that do not even taste that sweet.

Fructose, the fruit-sugar part of the syrup, is processed mostly in the liver. Research has tied very high intakes of fructose to fatty liver. Note the words: very high intakes. That is not the same as saying a normal diet causes it. The science here is about large amounts, and we should describe it that way. You can read a 2018 review of the research.

To make it real, look at one 12-ounce soda. It has about 39 grams of sugar. That is around 10 teaspoons. One bottle of soda blows past the whole day's limit for a woman. And most people do not stop at one.

The Princeton rat study

You may have heard that high fructose corn syrup is worse than sugar. This comes partly from a 2010 study at Princeton. Researchers fed rats high fructose corn syrup, and those rats gained more weight than rats fed the same number of calories as sucrose. You can read the study on PubMed.

Say this plainly: it was an animal study. It is debated among scientists. It does not prove the same thing happens in people. Rats are not humans, and a lab result is not a fact about your body.

The "added sugars" line on labels

Since 2020, the FDA has required an "Added Sugars" line on Nutrition Facts labels in the United States. This is useful. Before this change, you could not tell how much of the sugar in a food was added by the maker and how much was natural, like the sugar in plain milk or fruit. Now you can see the number. Use it.

What you can do

First, know where it hides. Soda, candy, and baked goods, yes. But also bread, ketchup, and salad dressing. Check the label on foods you would never call sweet. It is in more of the grocery store than you think.

Second, stop arguing with yourself about which sweetener is worse. Look at the grams instead. Compare grams of added sugar per serving, not just the sweetener name. Twelve grams is twelve grams, whatever the source. If the label says "high-fructose corn syrup," know it is not poison — it is just sugar in another form. Judge the amount, not the name. Watch serving sizes, too: a bottle that looks like one serving may list two or three on the label.

Third, drink mostly water. Pick one sugary drink a day to replace with water. That one change can cut a large share of your added sugar without any diet plan. Water costs almost nothing, has zero grams of added sugar, and it is always there. Soda becomes a treat, not a daily drink, and the sugar takes care of itself.
