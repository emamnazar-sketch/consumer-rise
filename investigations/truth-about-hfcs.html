SWEETENERS

The Truth About High Fructose Corn Syrup

Issue #428 min readMay 15, 2026

You grab a cold soda on a hot day. The first sip is perfect. Cold, sweet, sharp. You flip the bottle around. The label says high fructose corn syrup. Those four words have started more dinner-table arguments than almost any other ingredient. Is it poison? Is it just sugar with a bad name? Here is what we actually know.

How it took over

High fructose corn syrup was developed in 1957, when two chemists found a way to turn corn glucose into fructose. It stayed a lab curiosity for years. In the 1970s and 1980s, it took over the American soda supply. Soda makers switched to it. Candy makers switched to it. Soon it was everywhere.

It was not an accident. It was a business decision — and USDA data shows how completely the soda industry switched. By the 1990s, if you drank a soda in America, you were almost certainly drinking corn syrup.

Why the industry loves it

Three reasons. First, it is cheap — that is the main reason it spread so fast. Second, it is a liquid. That makes it easy to mix into soda and other drinks on a fast production line. Third, it keeps products moist on the shelf, so cookies and cakes stay soft longer. The FDA's HFCS questions and answers cover how ingredients like this are regulated.

Put those together and you get an ingredient that saves money at every step. Cheap to buy, easy to use, and it makes products last. For a food company, that is hard to beat. That is why it spread so fast.

The peak and the fall

US consumption of high fructose corn syrup peaked around 1999. Then it started to fall. The reason is simple. Soda sales dropped. People started drinking less soda, and the syrup went down with it. Watchdog groups like the Center for Science in the Public Interest pushed hard on soda during those years.

That tells you something important. The story of this ingredient is really the story of soda. When America cut back on soda, the syrup faded too. The ingredient did not get banned. People just bought less of what carried it.

What it does to your body

Here is where we need to be careful. The strongest evidence is not about corn syrup alone. It is about added sugar in general. High intake of added sugar is linked to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and heart disease. The American Heart Association sets a daily limit: no more than 36 grams of added sugar for men, and 25 grams for women. That is the ceiling, not the goal.

Fructose, the fruit-sugar part of the syrup, is processed mostly in the liver. Research has tied very high intakes of fructose to fatty liver. Note the words: very high intakes. That is not the same as saying a normal diet causes it. The science here is about large amounts, and we should describe it that way. You can read a 2018 review of the research.

To make it real, look at one 12-ounce soda. It has about 39 grams of sugar. That is around 10 teaspoons. One bottle of soda blows past the whole day's limit for a woman. And most people do not stop at one.

What you can do

First, know where it hides. Soda, candy, and baked goods, yes. But also bread, ketchup, and salad dressing. Check the label on foods you would never call sweet. It is in more of the grocery store than you think.

Second, compare the added-sugar grams. Two brands of bread can look the same and have very different sugar. Pick the lower one. Third, drink mostly water. That one habit does more than any label reading. Soda becomes a treat, not a daily drink, and the sugar takes care of itself.

Sources

  • USDA Economic Research Service: sweetener availability peaked in 1999
  • Coca-Cola nutrition facts: 39g sugar per 12 oz
  • American Heart Association: added sugars limits (men 36g/day, women 25g/day)
  • PubMed: Jensen et al. 2018 — fructose and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (review)
  • FDA: High Fructose Corn Syrup Questions and Answers
