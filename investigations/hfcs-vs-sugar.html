SWEETENERS

High Fructose Corn Syrup vs Sugar: The Real Difference

Issue #386 min readMarch 5, 2026

You are in the drink aisle. One soda says "made with real sugar" like that is a health claim. The bottle next to it lists high-fructose corn syrup. You wonder: is one actually better than the other?

Short answer: not really. Here is the longer one.

They Are Almost the Same Thing

High fructose corn syrup sounds like a chemical monster. Table sugar sounds natural and safe. The truth is less dramatic. HFCS-55 — the kind in most soda — is about 55 percent fructose and 45 percent glucose. Table sugar, also called sucrose, is 50 percent fructose and 50 percent glucose, with the two bound together. The FDA's food pages are a good starting point if you want to read more about sweeteners.

Your body handles them in very similar ways. Once they are digested, the difference is tiny. And here is the part that matters most: neither one is healthy.

Food makers like HFCS because it is cheap and easy to mix into drinks. That is a business reason, not a health reason. Do not confuse the two.

The Real Problem Is Total Added Sugar

The real problem is not which sweetener is on the label. It is how much added sugar you get in a day. It adds up fast: a soda here, a sweet coffee there, dessert after dinner.

The American Heart Association sets clear limits on added sugar: no more than 36 grams a day for men — about 9 teaspoons — and no more than 25 grams for women — about 6 teaspoons. Children should get even less.

For children, the numbers are lower, and the stakes feel higher. Kids who drink a lot of sugary drinks tend to take in more calories overall. That is the pattern worth breaking.

3 in 5 Americans over age 2 take in more added sugar than recommended, says the CDC — and most do not know it. The sugar hides in bread, sauces, yogurt, and drinks that do not even taste that sweet.

The Princeton Rat Study

You may have heard that high fructose corn syrup is worse than sugar. This comes partly from a 2010 study at Princeton. Researchers led by Bocarsly fed rats high fructose corn syrup, and those rats gained more weight than rats fed the same number of calories as sucrose. You can search for the study on PubMed.

Say this plainly: it was an animal study. It is debated among scientists. It does not prove the same thing happens in people. Rats are not humans, and a lab result is not a fact about your body.

What About "Natural" Sugar?

Honey, agave, and cane sugar get marketed as better choices. Your body treats their sugars much the same way. They count toward the same daily limits. Natural on the label does not mean unlimited in your diet.

Labels Now Show Added Sugar

Since 2020, the FDA has required an "Added Sugars" line on Nutrition Facts labels in the United States. This is useful. Before this change, you could not tell how much of the sugar in a food was added by the maker and how much was natural, like the sugar in plain milk or fruit. Read more on the FDA's food pages.

Now you can see the number. Use it.

What You Can Do

Stop arguing with yourself about which sweetener is worse. Look at the grams instead. Pick one sugary drink a day to replace with water. That one change can cut a large share of your added sugar without any diet plan.

  • Compare grams of added sugar per serving, not just the sweetener name. Twelve grams is twelve grams, whatever the source.
  • If the label says "high-fructose corn syrup," know it is not poison — it is just sugar in another form. Judge the amount, not the name.
  • Watch serving sizes. A bottle that looks like one serving may list two or three on the label.
  • Water is the cheapest fix. It costs almost nothing, has zero grams of added sugar, and it is always there.
